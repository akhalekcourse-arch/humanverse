Research 01 — The Chemistry of Human Bones

Research Question: What is the molecular composition of bone extracellular matrix and how is mineralization regulated?

Background: Bone is a composite tissue providing mechanical strength while participating in mineral homeostasis.

Hypothesis: Bone matrix consists predominantly of type I collagen and hydroxyapatite, with mineralization tightly controlled by osteoblast activity and systemic hormones.

Methodology: Literature review of open educational and peer-reviewed sources (OpenStax, NCBI Bookshelf).

Evidence & Results: Organic phase (~30%): primarily type I collagen. Inorganic phase (~70%): hydroxyapatite crystals [Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂]. Osteoblasts secrete osteoid that subsequently mineralizes. PTH, calcitonin, and vitamin D regulate calcium-phosphate balance.

Discussion: The composite structure explains both flexibility (collagen) and hardness (mineral). Dysregulation leads to conditions such as osteoporosis or rickets.

Limitations: This is a literature-based synthesis. No original experimental data were generated.

Conclusion: Bone chemistry reflects a precisely regulated balance between organic matrix and mineral deposition.

References: OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology (2022); NCBI Bookshelf — Bone Histology and Physiology.

Research 02 — Oxygen Transport by Hemoglobin

Research Question: How does hemoglobin structure enable efficient oxygen loading in lungs and unloading in tissues?

Background: Most oxygen in blood is carried bound to hemoglobin rather than dissolved in plasma.

Methodology: Synthesis of standard respiratory and hematology physiology sources.

Evidence: Hemoglobin is a tetramer (α₂β₂) with four heme groups. Cooperative binding produces the characteristic sigmoid oxygen-dissociation curve. The Bohr effect (decreased pH or increased CO₂) shifts the curve rightward, facilitating unloading. Approximately 98.5% of arterial oxygen is hemoglobin-bound.

Limitations: Literature review only.

References: OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology; standard medical physiology references.

Research 03 — Mitochondrial ATP Production

Research Question: How do mitochondria generate the majority of cellular ATP from glucose?

Methodology: Review of cellular respiration pathways in standard biochemistry texts.

Evidence: Glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. The electron transport chain establishes a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane; ATP synthase harnesses proton re-entry. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor. Net yield ≈ 30–32 ATP per glucose under aerobic conditions.

Limitations: Literature-based; exact ATP yield can vary with shuttle systems and conditions.

References: OpenStax Biology; Lehninger Principles of Biochemistry (conceptual sections).
